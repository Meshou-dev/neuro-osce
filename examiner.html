Neurological emergencies — 4-station clinical circuit

A teaching circuit modelled on the Saudi Board Final Clinical Examination. Each case is a single integrated station run as a structured oral: the candidate is given a scenario, requests data and images as needed, and is examined against a marked assessment sheet. Share the Candidate file over Zoom; ask your questions and mark here on your own screen.

Format: structured oral / OSCE
Stations: 4 (one integrated station each)
Time: 10 minutes per station
Marking: 2 / 1 / 0 per item, four domains
Standard: borderline regression
Running it: open the Candidate page in a separate tab — that is what you screen-share over Zoom. Keep this Examiner console private: in Zoom, share only the candidate window, never your whole screen. Advance the candidate's station from that window; ask your questions and mark here on the sheet.
Examiner conduct — read before you start
Standardised conduct (modelled on the real exam):
  • Interact professionally but neutrally — do not teach, coach, hint, or give feedback during the station.
  • Release history, examination findings, results and imaging only when the candidate asks for them (use the reveal buttons).
  • You manage time. To keep pace you may say: “In the interests of time, let's move to the next question” — reassure the candidate this has no bearing on their score.
  • Ask the structured questions on the mark sheet. Score each item 2 = done adequately · 1 = partially / attempted · 0 = not done.
  • Also give an independent global rating (Clear Fail → Clear Pass). Checklist and global together feed borderline-regression standard setting (the usual OSCE method; illustrative here).

Domains assessed (SCFHS): Data-Gathering Skills · Reasoning & Analytical Skills · Decision-Making Skills · Professional Attitude. Framework: CanMEDS.

Data-Gathering Skills0 / 6
1
What must you establish on history?
Model: time last-known-well (here 90 min); thrombolysis contraindication screen — recent surgery/trauma, MI, bleeding disorder/anticoagulation, prior stroke or intracranial haemorrhage; hypoglycaemia symptoms.
2
Interpret the examination and localise.
Model: left (dominant) MCA territory — right face/arm>leg weakness, expressive dysphasia, left gaze deviation, right hemianopia/inattention; AF → likely cardioembolic. NIHSS limitations: weighted to LOC; scores higher for dominant/left-hemisphere strokes; over-scores prior deficits.
3
What investigations, and in what order?
Model: POC glucose; non-contrast CT first (exclude haemorrhage); CT angiography for large-vessel occlusion (± CT perfusion for late windows); CBC/platelets, coagulation/INR, U&E, ECG. Do not delay lysis for labs unless bleeding risk.
Reasoning & Analytical Skills0 / 6
4
Interpret the CT head.
Model: hyperdense left MCA sign, no haemorrhage, early loss of grey–white differentiation in the left MCA territory. CTA: left M1 occlusion (large-vessel occlusion).
5
Is he a thrombolysis candidate? Justify; agent and dose.
Model: yes — within 4.5 h, no haemorrhage, no contraindication. Alteplase 0.9 mg/kg (max 90 mg; 10% bolus, rest over 60 min) or tenecteplase 0.25 mg/kg bolus (max 25 mg). CRITICAL — imaging before lysis
6
Name the main contraindications to thrombolysis.
Model: Absolute: intracranial haemorrhage; active internal bleeding; uncontrollable BP >185/110; platelets <100; INR >1.7 / therapeutic anticoagulation; ischaemic stroke or serious head trauma <3 months. Relative / individualised: recent major surgery (~10–14 days); recent GI/GU bleed; DOAC within 48 h. Hypoglycaemia is a stroke mimic — correct the glucose and reassess rather than exclude outright.
Decision-Making Skills0 / 8
7
BP is 182/98 — how do you manage it around thrombolysis?
Model: must be <185/110 before lysis (acceptable now; treat if it rises — labetalol/nicardipine); keep <180/105 for 24 h after. If not reperfusing, permissive hypertension and treat only if >220/120.
8
CTA shows an M1 occlusion — what now?
Model: refer for mechanical thrombectomy (proximal LVO). Window ≤6 h; up to 24 h in selected patients with favourable perfusion/clinical mismatch (DAWN/DEFUSE-3); selected large-core infarcts now also benefit (recent trials). Give bridging thrombolysis if eligible; don't delay transfer.
9
During the infusion he develops severe headache and drops his GCS. And 1 h later, lip swelling?
Model: stop the infusion; urgent non-contrast CT; send CBC, coagulation and fibrinogen; reverse (cryoprecipitate guided by fibrinogen ± platelets/tranexamic acid) and involve neurosurgery (suspect haemorrhage). Oro-lingual angio-oedema: stop/secure airway, IV methylprednisolone 125–250 mg + antihistamine (± adrenaline if progressing).
10
Post-thrombolysis care and secondary prevention.
Model: stroke-unit/monitored bed 24 h; NPO then swallow screen; IV normal saline (no dextrose); aspirin after 24 h + repeat CT first; intermittent pneumatic compression from admission and pharmacological VTE prophylaxis after 24 h; anticoagulate for AF by infarct size; statin.
Professional Attitude0 / 2
11
How do you consent the family for thrombolysis?
Model: clear, empathetic, time-critical explanation — ~6% risk of symptomatic haemorrhage vs meaningful benefit; shared decision; document.

Data-Gathering Skills0 / 6
1
What is the diagnosis and what makes it a “crisis”?
Model: myasthenic crisis — MG exacerbation causing respiratory failure or inability to protect the airway, requiring ventilatory support. Bulbar + respiratory weakness are the danger signs.
2
How do you assess respiratory status at the bedside?
Model: serial FVC and NIF/MIP, single-breath count, cough strength, neck-flexion strength, orthopnoea, sentence length. CRITICAL
3
Why not simply rely on the oxygen saturation / ABG?
Model: in neuromuscular failure the SpO₂ falls and the CO₂ rises only late — a normal saturation with an FVC of 0.9 L is a patient about to arrest. The trend of FVC/NIF is what matters.
Reasoning & Analytical Skills0 / 4
4
What precipitated this, and what will you screen for?
Model: infection (her pneumonia) + a precipitating drug (the macrolide). Also surgery, pregnancy, tapering immunosuppression, thyroid dysfunction. Drugs to avoid: aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, macrolides, β-blockers, IV magnesium, procainamide, quinine, checkpoint inhibitors.
5
How would you distinguish this from a cholinergic crisis?
Model: cholinergic = over-treatment → muscarinic excess (SLUDGE, miosis, bradycardia, sweating) plus nicotinic features (fasciculations, weakness). Here: normal pupils, no fasciculations, clear precipitant → myasthenic. When in doubt, secure the airway and hold the anticholinesterase.
Decision-Making Skills0 / 6
6
At what point would you secure the airway?
Model: the “20·30·40 rule” (FVC < 20 mL/kg ≈ <1 L, NIF weaker than −30 cmH₂O, MEP < 40) is an escalation / ICU-assessment guide, not an automatic trigger — decide with the trend, cough, secretions and bulbar function. This patient needs intubation for bulbar failure, pooled secretions, weak cough and rising CO₂; the numbers reinforce it.
7
Outline your definitive management.
Model: ICU; airway/ventilatory support; treat the pneumonia and stop the offending drug; IVIG 2 g/kg over 2–5 days or plasma exchange. Corticosteroids to follow — beware transient early worsening.
8
What do you do with the pyridostigmine peri-intubation?
Model: hold it when intubated (reduces secretions, avoids cholinergic confusion); restart later.
Professional Attitude0 / 2
9
Communication & escalation.
Model: early, clear escalation to ICU/anaesthesia; explains the situation to the patient/family; structured hand-over.

Examinee assessment sheet — Station 3 (Optic neuritis / MS)

Data-Gathering Skills0 / 6
1
What are the key confirmatory signs, and the diagnosis?
Model: left optic neuritis — subacute painful monocular loss (worse on eye movement), RAPD, red/colour desaturation, central scotoma; fundus often normal.
2
Demonstrate how you elicit a RAPD.
Model: swinging-flashlight test in a dim room — when light swings to the affected eye, both pupils paradoxically dilate (reduced afferent input). CRITICAL sign
3
What investigations would you request?
Model: MRI brain + orbits with gadolinium; acuity/colour/fields ± OCT; bloods to exclude mimics; if atypical → AQP4/MOG antibodies; consider LP for oligoclonal bands.
Reasoning & Analytical Skills0 / 6
4
Interpret the MRI and link it to MS.
Model: left optic nerve T2 change; brain shows periventricular ovoid lesions / Dawson's fingers → dissemination in space. Establish dissemination in time with a gadolinium (post-contrast T1) study or the earlier clinical episode, then apply the McDonald criteria (the 2024 revision now counts the optic nerve as a fifth region); CSF oligoclonal bands can substitute for dissemination in time.
5
What atypical features would make you suspect NMOSD/MOGAD?
Model: red flags — severe or bilateral loss, very poor recovery, marked/persistent pain, simultaneous or recurrent episodes. AQP4-NMOSD: chiasmal/posterior involvement, severe residual loss. MOGAD: bilateral, longitudinally extensive anterior nerve, disc swelling, perineural enhancement. Send AQP4 and MOG antibodies; PLEX if severe or steroid-refractory.
6
What is the prognosis of optic neuritis?
Model: most recover good vision over weeks; it is a common first demyelinating event and raises MS risk (stratified by baseline MRI lesions).
Decision-Making Skills0 / 4
7
What is the acute treatment?
Model: IV methylprednisolone 1 g/day for 3–5 days (± short oral taper) — speeds recovery. Avoid standard-dose oral prednisone alone (ONTT: higher recurrence). PLEX if severe/steroid-refractory. CRITICAL
8
What is the ongoing plan?
Model: refer to MS/neuro-ophthalmology; discuss disease-modifying therapy; counsel and safety-net; monitor recovery.
Professional Attitude0 / 2
9
How do you counsel a young patient facing a possible MS diagnosis?
Model: honest, staged, hopeful information; checks understanding; avoids premature labelling before criteria met; arranges support and follow-up.

Data-Gathering Skills0 / 4
1
Define status epilepticus.
Model: ≥5 minutes of continuous seizure, or ≥2 seizures without recovery of consciousness in between (operational, convulsive).
2
Walk through your first five minutes.
Model: ABC, high-flow O₂, airway adjuncts/suction, two IV cannulae, monitoring; capillary glucose (thiamine before glucose if malnourished/alcohol — but never delay dextrose for confirmed hypoglycaemia); give the first-line benzodiazepine immediately and in parallel (he has already seized ~8 min — see next item); send bloods (glucose, U&E, Ca/Mg, AED levels, tox, ABG); time the seizure. CRITICAL
Reasoning & Analytical Skills0 / 4
3
What causes must you actively exclude, and how?
Model: here AED non-adherence/withdrawal. Also hypoglycaemia, hyponatraemia, hypocalcaemia, CNS infection, stroke/haemorrhage, trauma, toxins/alcohol withdrawal, eclampsia. Work-up: glucose/electrolytes, AED levels, tox, CT head, LP if infection suspected, EEG; empiric aciclovir/antibiotics if meningoencephalitis likely.
4
Why is timing so important, and what is the commonest error?
Model: longer seizures are harder to stop and cause neuronal injury; escalate by the clock without delay. Commonest error: under-dosing benzodiazepines and not moving to second-line.
Decision-Making Skills0 / 8
5
First-line drug, route and dose?
Model: a benzodiazepine — lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg IV (max 4 mg/dose, may repeat once), or IM midazolam 10 mg if no IV, or diazepam 0.15–0.2 mg/kg IV (max 10 mg). CRITICAL
6
Still fitting after benzodiazepines — what next?
Model: second-line IV antiseizure medication at full loading dose — levetiracetam 60 mg/kg (max 4.5 g), valproate 40 mg/kg (max 3 g), or fosphenytoin 20 mg PE/kg (max 1.5 g PE). ESETT: broadly equivalent.
7
It is now refractory. Plan?
Model: rapid-sequence intubation + continuous anaesthetic infusion (midazolam, propofol, or pentobarbital); ICU with continuous EEG to confirm suppression / detect non-convulsive status.
8
Interpret the EEG and its role here.
Model: generalised epileptiform discharges; after paralysis/anaesthesia, cEEG is essential to detect ongoing non-convulsive status and titrate to seizure suppression (or burst-suppression).
Professional Attitude0 / 2
9
Team leadership & communication.
Model: clear team roles, closed-loop instructions, early ICU/anaesthesia call, and family update once stable.
